Claude Shannon, American mathematician, electrical engineer, computer scientist, cryptographer and inventor known as the "father of information theory" and the man who laid the foundations of the Information Age, deliberately ignored meaning. In his foundational work on information theory, he wrote:

"Frequently the messages have meaning; that is they refer to or are correlated according to some system with certain physical or conceptual entities. These semantic aspects of communication are irrelevant to the engineering problem."

This was brilliant strategy. By setting meaning aside, Shannon could focus his considerable intellect on the fundamental challenge of communication: maximizing the survival of bits traveling from source to destination. His semantic-free approach gave us the mathematical foundations for nearly every communication technology we rely on today—the internet, cell phones, GPS satellites, digital television. His error-correction codes ensure your video streams arrive intact despite traveling through noisy cables and wireless signals.

But Shannon's deliberate blindness to meaning has left us vulnerable. We have a rich vocabulary for discussing how many bits survive the journey from A to B, but no mathematical framework for discussing which bits deserve to survive.

This gap has become dangerous. A handful of private companies now wield recommender algorithms that erode billions of people's ability to make decisions free from manipulation. No private entity should decide what version of reality gets prioritized—especially when their design serves profit rather than democratic values like freedom, equality, and rule of law.

The stakes are about to escalate dramatically. Billions of AI agents will soon live alongside human intelligence, teaching our children, coaching the ambitious, counseling the vulnerable, and advising our leaders. Who gets to decide what values these systems embody and what facts ground their understanding? These decisions demand mathematical rigor around truth itself—tools to preserve accuracy against tampering and subversion.

Introducing Semantic Truth to Information Theory

As a first step toward enriching our vocabulary, I propose reintroducing semantics to communication theory—specifically, the semantic truthfulness of the bits Shannon wants to see survive from A to B.

We must distinguish between fidelity and semantic truth. They belong to separate domains: fidelity is an engineering property, truth is an epistemic property. Fidelity measures how many bits survived transmission. Truth measures how accurately those bits reflect external reality. [1]

To build intuition, let's examine a simplified model with core assumptions:

  • The destination cannot error-correct lossy messages

  • The destination cannot independently verify message truth

  • Mistruth originates only at the source, not from channel noise

In this model, both fidelity and truth are binary variables:

  • Fidelity: Perfect (F = 1) or Imperfect (0 <= F < 1)

  • Truth: Complete (T = 1) or Incomplete (0 <= T < 1)

This creates four canonical scenarios for any communication system.

Four Scenarios: Truth Meets Fidelity

Consider a simple example: communicating my height (6 feet 4 inches).

Scenario 1: Gold Standard (Perfect channel, true claim) Source truthfully states "Alex is 6'4"" → Perfect transmission → Destination receives accurate information Result: Truthful knowledge transfer succeeds

Scenario 2: Perfect Propaganda (Perfect channel, false claim)
Source falsely states "Alex is 10'5"" → Perfect transmission → Destination receives misinformation with perfect clarity Result: Efficient deception.

Scenario 3: Broken Truth (Imperfect channel, true claim) Source truthfully states "Alex is 6'4"" → Partial loss → Destination receives "Alex is 6'" Result: Truth becomes casualty of poor infrastructure

Scenario 4: Broken Lies (Imperfect channel, false claim) Source falsely states "Alex is 10'5"" → Garbled transmission → Destination receives corrupted misinformation Result: Garbled falsehood.

The Democratic Imperative

Democratic societies should maximize Scenario 1 for institutions that inform citizens—media organizations, universities, scientific bodies. Unfortunately, truth often requires effort to spread and inconveniences vested interests, leaving it typically relegated to Scenario 3.

The authoritarian playbook exploits this dynamic: convince citizens that establishment institutions operate in Scenario 2 while claiming to be the only source of Scenario 1 communication. By sowing doubt in democratic institutions, authoritarians position themselves as sole truth-tellers. Once in power, they fill those same institutions with loyalists—becoming the very thing they claimed to fight.

From Theory to Practice: The Golden Dataset Problem

To mature this theory, we could model truth as a continuous probabilistic variable, measuring semantic distance against curated "golden datasets" representing ground truth. This mirrors how AI systems are currently evaluated for accuracy.

This approach works well for corporate AI agents grounded in objective data like sales figures. But what happens when AI agents operate in socio-politically charged domains?

Consider a Teacher AI responsible for educating students about January 6th. Depending on your political perspective, you might contest the other side's right to determine the golden dataset that grounds the system. This tug-of-war over truth already exists in schools around topics like evolution and gender identity. But AI agents escalate the stakes—they can scale rapidly, operate with perceived neutrality, and shape beliefs with quiet authority.

Without safeguards, AI risks becoming not just a mirror of political polarization, but an accelerant.

A Democratic Framework for Truth

Any technology that aims to encode truth in communication systems used by billions must operate under democratic governance. Instead of allowing single ministries, parties, or platforms to curate grounding datasets, democracies should require three safeguards:

1. Source Diversity by Design Grounding data must draw simultaneously from peer-reviewed science, independent newsrooms, civil society archives, and transparent government records. No single feed can be altered without discrepancies becoming visible across the network.

2. Open Auditing Tools
The algorithms that score messages against multi-source libraries—the truth coefficients—must be published, inspectable, and reproducible by outside researchers. Public yardsticks make quiet tampering far harder.

3. Rotating Multi-Stakeholder Stewardship Oversight boards should include statisticians, journalists, domain experts, and citizen representatives, with staggered terms and clear procedures for contesting or updating ground truth determinations.

By embedding redundancy, transparency, and shared custody into the infrastructure feeding AI systems, democracies can maintain high channel fidelity while preventing any actor from dragging semantic truth toward zero. This institutional "triple lock" offers practical countermeasures to information warfare and represents the first real test of truth-fidelity modeling beyond pure theory.

The Path Forward

This isn't merely an abstract modeling exercise—it's a call to action. If democracies are to survive algorithmic information warfare, we need more than encryption protocols and privacy laws. We need to revive the social contract around truth itself, with tools to preserve it throughout our communication systems.

Not in the naive sense that we'll agree on every interpretation, but in the foundational sense that we can agree on how disagreement is handled. Human society must learn to collaborate again through shared values and shared understanding of facts. Only this way can we address global challenges—extremism, climate change, poverty, disease, and the creeping authoritarianism that thrives where common truths have eroded.

Shannon gave us the engineering foundation for the information age. Now it's our responsibility to complete his work by bringing meaning back into the equation. The survival of democratic discourse may depend on it.

[1] Here we are drawing from the “Correspondence Theory of Truth” advocated by Bertrand Russell and Moore early in the 20th century - “does this proposition match the relevant slice of the world?”. To be extra rigorous we could also introduce “falsifiability” into the picture, as another epistemic property of the bits being transmitted in a communication system. If bits are indeed falsifiable, that means proposition encoded in the bits can be tested against external evidence. Truth and falsehood are then an outcome of the test. Ideally we want all bits to be falsifiable, but many bits are simply not amenable to this rigor - opinions, feelings, jokes, thought experiments.
